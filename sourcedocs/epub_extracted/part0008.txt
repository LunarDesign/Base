What is the primary control for ventilation? a) Baroreceptors b) Peripheral chemoreceptors c) Diaphragm function d) Central control in the brain stem Correct Answer: d) Central control in the brain stem Explanation: The brain stem is the primary control for ventilation. It senses blood pH levels and stimulates ventilation in response to changes in pH, such as acidosis, by increasing the rate and/or depth of breathing. 2. In a chronic PaCO₂ retainer, what is the consequence of correcting the PaO₂ to normal levels? a) Decreased drive to breathe b) No effect on breathing pattern c) Increase in respiratory rate d) Improvement in ventilation Correct Answer: a) Decreased drive to breathe Explanation: Chronic PaCO₂ retainers rely on mild hypoxemia for their ventilatory drive. Correcting the PaO₂ to normal levels can reduce their drive to breathe, potentially leading to respiratory depression. 3. What is the normal minute ventilation for a healthy adult? a) 8 L/minute b) 4 L/minute c) 2 L/minute d) 6 L/minute Correct Answer: b) 4 L/minute Explanation: The normal minute ventilation for a healthy adult is approximately 4 L/min. Minute ventilation is the product of tidal volume (Vt) and respiratory rate (RR). 4. What is the primary muscle involved in ventilation? a) Sternocleidomastoid b) Intercostal muscles c) Abdominal muscles d) Diaphragm Correct Answer: d) Diaphragm Explanation: The diaphragm is the primary muscle of ventilation. Any condition affecting its function, such as deconditioning or hypophosphatemia, can impair ventilation. 5. Which of the following positions is optimal for ventilation? a) Supine position b) Prone position c) Upright sitting position d) Lying flat on the back Correct Answer: c) Upright sitting position Explanation: The upright sitting position is optimal for ventilation because it allows for maximum expansion of the lungs and better gas exchange. The supine position is not ideal for patients in respiratory distress. 6. What is the primary cause of alveolar dead space ventilation? a) Pulmonary embolus b) Asthma c) Atelectasis d) Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease Correct Answer: a) Pulmonary embolus Explanation: Alveolar dead space occurs when there is no blood flow past the alveoli, as seen in conditions such as pulmonary embolus. This leads to ventilation without perfusion in those areas. 7. Which of the following is considered a normal volume for anatomical dead space? a) 6 mL/kg of tidal volume b) 4 mL/kg of tidal volume c) 1 mL/kg of tidal volume d) 2 mL/kg of tidal volume Correct Answer: d) 2 mL/kg of tidal volume Explanation: Anatomical dead space is approximately 2 mL/kg of tidal volume. It represents the volume of air that does not participate in gas exchange, from the nose down to the alveoli. 8. How does a pulmonary embolus affect physiological dead space ventilation? a) Decreases the total dead space b) Increases alveolar dead space c) Decreases ventilation d) Reduces perfusion to all alveoli Correct Answer: b) Increases alveolar dead space Explanation: A pulmonary embolus blocks blood flow to certain alveoli, causing an increase in alveolar dead space. This leads to ventilation without perfusion in the affected areas. 9. What is the normal ventilation/perfusion ratio (V/Q ratio) for healthy lungs? a) 0.4 b) 0.6 c) 1.0 d) 0.8 Correct Answer: d) 0.8 Explanation: The normal V/Q ratio is 0.8, which is derived from 4 L of ventilation per minute (V) and 5 L of perfusion per minute (Q). This ratio indicates an ideal balance between ventilation and blood flow. 10. In a patient with low cardiac output, what impact could this have on the ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) ratio? a) Increased V/Q ratio b) Equal increase in ventilation and perfusion c) No impact on the V/Q ratio d) Decreased V/Q ratio Correct Answer: d) Decreased V/Q ratio Explanation: Low cardiac output can lead to decreased perfusion, which may result in a decreased V/Q ratio, affecting gas exchange despite normal ventilation. 11. In an upright position, where is most pulmonary blood located? a) Upper lung lobes b) Middle lung lobes c) Lower lung lobes d) Equally distributed Correct Answer: c) Lower lung lobes Explanation: In the upright position, gravity causes most pulmonary blood to pool in the lower lung lobes, resulting in greater perfusion in those areas. 12. What is the primary clinical implication of turning a patient with right lung pneumonia onto their right side? a) Increased perfusion to the "good" lung b) Improved oxygenation c) No change in oxygenation d) Potential worsening of hypoxemia Correct Answer: d) Potential worsening of hypoxemia Explanation: Turning a patient with right lung pneumonia onto their right side ("bad" lung) directs more blood to the affected lung, which is poorly ventilated, potentially worsening hypoxemia. 13. How does prone positioning improve oxygenation in severe hypoxemia? a) By enhancing the tidal volume of breathing b) By evenly distributing blood flow to all lung lobes c) By increasing ventilation to posterior alveoli d) By improving perfusion to under-perfused anterior alveoli Correct Answer: d) By improving perfusion to under-perfused anterior alveoli Explanation: Prone positioning improves oxygenation by enhancing perfusion to previously under-perfused anterior alveoli, optimizing the V/Q ratio. 14. What characterizes a normal V/Q ratio? a) A mismatch between ventilation and perfusion b) Equal problems with ventilation and perfusion c) Absent gas exchange on room air d) Balanced ventilation and perfusion with normal gas exchange on room air Correct Answer: d) Balanced ventilation and perfusion with normal gas exchange on room air Explanation: A normal V/Q ratio occurs when ventilation and perfusion are balanced, resulting in effective gas exchange on room air. 15. How does a V/Q mismatch typically manifest clinically? a) Normal oxygenation with increased respiratory rate b) Hypercapnia c) Cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen therapy d) Hypoxemia on room air Correct Answer: d) Hypoxemia on room air Explanation: A V/Q mismatch causes hypoxemia on room air, which is generally correctable with supplemental oxygen until the underlying cause is identified and treated. 16. What defines a shunt in pulmonary physiology? a) Oxygenation of all blood in the lungs b) Ventilation without perfusion c) Perfusion without ventilation d) Equal ventilation and perfusion Correct Answer: c) Perfusion without ventilation Explanation: A shunt occurs when blood moves from the right side of the heart to the left without being oxygenated, leading to perfusion without ventilation. 17. Why is the normal oxygen saturation on room air not 100%? a) Cardiac output is unevenly distributed in the lungs. b) Thebesian veins contribute a physiological shunt. c) Hypoxemia is normal at baseline. d) Ventilation does not reach all lung units. Correct Answer: b) Thebesian veins contribute a physiological shunt. Explanation: The Thebesian veins create a physiological shunt by draining venous blood directly into the left atrium, slightly lowering the oxygen saturation on room air. 18. Which of the following is an example of an anatomical shunt? a) Alveolar dead space b) Ventricular septal defect c) Hypoventilation d) Pulmonary embolism Correct Answer: b) Ventricular septal defect Explanation: An anatomical shunt involves structural abnormalities, such as a ventricular septal defect, which allow blood to bypass the oxygenation process in the lungs. 19. What is the first step in treating a V/Q mismatch? a) Providing supplemental oxygen b) Initiating mechanical ventilation c) Administering antibiotics d) Starting anticoagulants Correct Answer: a) Providing supplemental oxygen Explanation: The initial step in treating a V/Q mismatch is providing supplemental oxygen to correct hypoxemia while identifying and treating the underlying cause. 20. Why does 100% FiO₂ fail to correct hypoxemia in a shunt? a) The alveoli are overventilated. b) Blood bypasses the lungs and does not participate in gas exchange. c) The shunt prevents oxygen from reaching under-perfused areas. d) The shunt causes perfusion to exceed ventilation. Correct Answer: b) Blood bypasses the lungs and does not participate in gas exchange. Explanation: In a shunt, blood bypasses the lungs entirely and does not undergo gas exchange, rendering 100% FiO₂ ineffective in correcting hypoxemia. 21. What is the hallmark of a pathologic shunt in ARDS? a) Normal oxygenation despite high FiO₂ b) Blood passing through the lungs without getting oxygenated c) Blood bypassing the lungs entirely d) Increased PaO₂ and SaO₂ levels Correct Answer: b) Blood passing through the lungs without getting oxygenated Explanation: In ARDS, a pathologic shunt occurs when blood passes through the lungs but does not participate in gas exchange, resulting in refractory hypoxemia. 22. What is the primary benefit of applying positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) in a patient with a shunt? a) Increased alveolar collapse b) Decreased cardiac output c) Prevention of alveolar atelectasis d) Reduction in alveolar ventilation Correct Answer: c) Prevention of alveolar atelectasis Explanation: PEEP decreases alveolar surface tension, preventing collapse (atelectasis), and recruits additional alveoli for gas exchange, improving oxygenation. 23. Why is it crucial to decrease FiO₂ after adding PEEP? a) To increase cardiac workload b) To improve oxygen delivery to tissues c) To enhance alveolar collapse d) To reduce oxygen toxicity risk Correct Answer: d) To reduce oxygen toxicity risk Explanation: High FiO₂ levels over prolonged periods can cause oxygen toxicity. Adding PEEP helps improve oxygenation, allowing FiO₂ to be reduced safely. 24. Which clinical finding indicates anaerobic metabolism in critically ill patients? a) Increased PaO₂ b) Normal SaO₂ c) Low hemoglobin levels d) Elevated lactate levels Correct Answer: d) Elevated lactate levels Explanation: Elevated lactate levels signify lactic acidosis, a result of anaerobic metabolism due to insufficient oxygen at the cellular level. 25. In critically ill patients, why is it insufficient to evaluate only PaO₂ and SaO₂? a) These parameters are unreliable for assessing hypoxemia. b) They do not reflect hemoglobin levels. c) They are irrelevant in oxygenation assessment. d) Oxygen delivery and utilization may still be impaired. Correct Answer: d) Oxygen delivery and utilization may still be impaired. Explanation: PaO₂ and SaO₂ indicate oxygen levels in the blood but do not account for oxygen delivery, utilization, or cellular oxygenation. 26. What is the significance of mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO₂)? a) Reflects alveolar ventilation efficiency b) Measures the oxygen content in arterial blood c) Indicates the severity of lactic acidosis d) Assesses the balance between oxygen delivery and consumption Correct Answer: d) Assesses the balance between oxygen delivery and consumption Explanation: SvO₂ provides insight into the balance between oxygen delivery and consumption, helping identify states of oxygen supply-demand mismatch. 27. Which parameter indicates the total amount of oxygen in the blood, both bound to hemoglobin and dissolved in plasma? a) PaO₂ b) SaO₂ c) SvO₂ d) CaO₂ Correct Answer: d) CaO₂ Explanation: CaO₂ represents the total oxygen content in the blood, including hemoglobin-bound and dissolved oxygen. 28. What does an increased alveolar-arterial (A-a) gradient suggest? a) Low hemoglobin levels b) A problem with oxygen diffusion or ventilation-perfusion mismatch c) Normal oxygenation d) Adequate gas exchange Correct Answer: b) A problem with oxygen diffusion or ventilation-perfusion mismatch Explanation: An increased A-a gradient indicates impaired oxygen transfer from the alveoli to the blood, commonly seen in hypoxemia due to diffusion or V/Q mismatch issues. 29. Which clinical condition can cause lactic acidosis despite normal PaO₂ and SaO₂? a) ARDS b) Sepsis or septic shock c) Pulmonary embolism d) Severe anemia Correct Answer: b) Sepsis or septic shock Explanation: In sepsis or septic shock, oxygen utilization at the cellular level is impaired, leading to anaerobic metabolism and lactic acidosis, even if PaO₂ and SaO₂ are normal. 30. What does oxygen consumption (VO₂) measure? a) Total oxygen content in the blood b) Oxygen delivered to tissues per minute c) Efficiency of gas exchange in the lungs d) The amount of oxygen utilized by tissues per minute Correct Answer: d) The amount of oxygen utilized by tissues per minute Explanation: VO₂ represents the amount of oxygen used by tissues per minute, reflecting the metabolic activity and oxygen demand of the body. 31. What is the primary goal for SpO₂ in most critically ill patients? a) 90% or greater b) 80% or greater c) 95% or greater d) 85% or greater Correct Answer: a) 90% or greater Explanation: For most critically ill patients, the target SpO₂ is 90% or greater, typically achieved with supplemental oxygen, to ensure adequate oxygenation and aerobic metabolism. 32. What does an SpO₂ below 90% indicate about the PaO₂? a) PaO₂ is unaffected by SpO₂ b) PaO₂ is less than 60 mmHg c) PaO₂ is above 80 mmHg d) PaO₂ is between 70 and 80 mmHg Correct Answer: b) PaO₂ is less than 60 mmHg Explanation: When SpO₂ falls below 90%, the PaO₂ typically drops below 60 mmHg, impairing the ability of cells to maintain aerobic metabolism. 33. Which condition shifts the oxyhemoglobin-dissociation curve to the left? a) Alkalosis b) Acidosis c) Fever d) High 2,3-DPG Correct Answer: a) Alkalosis Explanation: Alkalosis, along with low PaCO₂, hypothermia, and low 2,3-DPG, shifts the curve to the left, making hemoglobin hold on to oxygen, reducing tissue oxygenation. 34. What is a characteristic of a rightward shift in the oxyhemoglobin-dissociation curve? a) Oxygen is tightly bound to hemoglobin b) Hemoglobin saturation increases at low oxygen levels c) Oxygen is more readily released to tissues d) SaO₂ is high but oxygen delivery to tissues is poor Correct Answer: c) Oxygen is more readily released to tissues Explanation: A rightward shift allows hemoglobin to release oxygen more easily to tissues, improving oxygen delivery despite lower SaO₂ levels. 35. Which of the following causes a rightward shift in the oxyhemoglobin-dissociation curve? a) High PaCO₂ b) Hypothermia c) Alkalosis d) Low 2,3-DPG Correct Answer: a) High PaCO₂ Explanation: High PaCO₂, along with acidosis, fever, and high 2,3-DPG, causes a rightward shift, enhancing oxygen release to tissues. 36. What happens to oxygen delivery during a leftward shift in the oxyhemoglobin-dissociation curve? a) Normal oxygen utilization b) Increased tissue oxygenation c) Decreased oxygen release to tissues d) Enhanced aerobic metabolism Correct Answer: c) Decreased oxygen release to tissues Explanation: In a leftward shift, hemoglobin holds onto oxygen more tightly, reducing the release of oxygen to the tissues, which may impair tissue oxygenation. 37. Which clinical condition is associated with a leftward shift in the oxyhemoglobin-dissociation curve? a) Acidosis b) Hypothermia c) High 2,3-DPG d) Fever Correct Answer: b) Hypothermia Explanation: Hypothermia, along with alkalosis, low PaCO₂, and low 2,3-DPG, is associated with a leftward shift, impairing oxygen release to tissues. 38. What effect does a rightward shift have on SaO₂ and oxygen availability to tissues? a) Low SaO₂ with reduced oxygen availability b) High SaO₂ with improved oxygen release c) Low SaO₂ but oxygen is easily released to tissues d) High SaO₂ but oxygen is stuck to hemoglobin Correct Answer: c) Low SaO₂ but oxygen is easily released to tissues Explanation: A rightward shift lowers SaO₂ but facilitates the release of oxygen from hemoglobin to the tissues, improving oxygen availability. 39. Why is understanding the oxyhemoglobin-dissociation curve crucial in critical care? a) It guides oxygen therapy and management of acid-base imbalances. b) It measures hemoglobin levels in the blood. c) It determines the need for intubation in all patients. d) It predicts arterial blood pressure changes. Correct Answer: a) It guides oxygen therapy and management of acid-base imbalances. Explanation: The oxyhemoglobin-dissociation curve helps clinicians optimize oxygen therapy and manage conditions affecting oxygen affinity, such as acid-base imbalances. 40. Which factor does not shift the oxyhemoglobin-dissociation curve to the right? a) Low 2,3-DPG b) High PaCO₂ c) Acidosis d) Fever Correct Answer: a) Low 2,3-DPG Explanation: Low 2,3-DPG causes a leftward shift, while acidosis, fever, and high PaCO₂ promote a rightward shift, enhancing oxygen release to tissues. 41. What is the main effect of decreased 2,3-DPG on oxygen delivery to tissues? a) No effect on oxygen delivery b) Increased oxygen saturation at low PaO₂ c) Reduced oxygen availability to tissues d) Enhanced oxygen release to tissues Correct Answer: c) Reduced oxygen availability to tissues Explanation: Decreased 2,3-DPG results in hemoglobin holding onto oxygen more tightly, reducing its release to tissues and impairing oxygen availability. 42. Which condition is associated with increased 2,3-DPG levels? a) Multiple blood transfusions b) Hyperthyroidism c) Hypophosphatemia d) Hypothyroidism Correct Answer: b) Hyperthyroidism Explanation: Increased 2,3-DPG levels are seen in conditions such as hyperthyroidism, chronic hypoxemia, or anemia, which enhance oxygen release to tissues. 43. What is the approximate affinity of carbon monoxide (CO) for hemoglobin compared to oxygen? a) 300 times greater b) 150 times greater c) 230 times greater d) 100 times greater Correct Answer: c) 230 times greater Explanation: Carbon monoxide has approximately 230 times the affinity for hemoglobin as oxygen, which prevents oxygen from binding and leads to tissue hypoxia. 44. Why is pulse oximetry unreliable in carbon monoxide poisoning? a) It overestimates PaO₂ levels. b) It cannot distinguish between CO and oxygen saturation. c) It measures carbon dioxide levels instead of oxygen. d) It fails to detect hypoxia in all cases. Explanation: Pulse oximetry cannot differentiate between oxygen and carbon monoxide saturation, making it unreliable for monitoring oxygenation status in CO poisoning. 45. What is the primary treatment for carbon monoxide poisoning? a) 100% FiO₂ until carboxyhemoglobin levels are <10% b) High-dose corticosteroids c) Blood transfusion d) Administration of bronchodilators Correct Answer: a) 100% FiO₂ until carboxyhemoglobin levels are <10% Explanation: Treatment involves administering 100% FiO₂ to displace CO from hemoglobin and reduce carboxyhemoglobin levels below 10%. 47. In which situation is the use of a hyperbaric oxygen chamber indicated for carbon monoxide poisoning? a) Asymptomatic patients with CO exposure b) Severe symptoms or rapid treatment within 30 minutes c) Mild symptoms with carboxyhemoglobin <15% d) Symptoms resolve with room air oxygen therapy Correct Answer: b) Severe symptoms or rapid treatment within 30 minutes Explanation: A hyperbaric oxygen chamber is indicated for severe CO poisoning or when rapid treatment is needed, as it accelerates the dissociation of CO from hemoglobin. 48. Which of the following causes decreased 2,3-DPG levels? a) Multiple blood transfusions of banked blood b) Chronic heart failure c) Anemia d) Prolonged time at high altitude Correct Answer: a) Multiple blood transfusions of banked blood Explanation: Decreased 2,3-DPG levels occur with multiple transfusions of banked blood, as stored blood gradually loses its 2,3-DPG, reducing oxygen delivery to tissues. 49. What is the primary reason carbon monoxide poisoning leads to tissue hypoxia? a) Disruption of hemoglobin production b) Impaired cellular oxygen utilization c) Decreased cardiac output d) CO's high affinity for hemoglobin, preventing oxygen binding Correct Answer: d) CO's high affinity for hemoglobin, preventing oxygen binding Explanation: Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin with much higher affinity than oxygen, displacing oxygen and preventing its transport to tissues, resulting in hypoxia. 50. At what carboxyhemoglobin level does mortality exceed 50%? a) 15-40% b) 30-50% c) >70% d) 50-70% Correct Answer: d) 50-70% Explanation: Mortality rates exceed 50% when carboxyhemoglobin levels reach 50-70%, as oxygen delivery is critically impaired, leading to severe tissue hypoxia and organ failure. 51. What parameter is used to calculate static lung compliance? a) Tidal volume / plateau pressure (minus PEEP) b) Peak inspiratory pressure × PEEP c) Plateau pressure × tidal volume d) Tidal volume / peak inspiratory pressure (minus PEEP) Correct Answer: a) Tidal volume / plateau pressure (minus PEEP) Explanation: Static compliance is measured by dividing the tidal volume by the plateau pressure (minus PEEP), reflecting the elastic properties of the lungs. 52.
