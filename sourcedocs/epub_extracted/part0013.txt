What is the most common cause of upper GI bleeding? a) Peptic ulcer disease b) Mallory-Weiss tear c) Stress ulcers d) Esophageal varices Correct Answer: a) Peptic ulcer disease Explanation: Peptic ulcer disease is responsible for approximately 50% of upper GI bleeding cases, making it the most common cause. 3. A patient presents with lower GI bleeding. Which of the following conditions is a potential cause? a) Esophageal varices b) Crohn’s disease c) Peptic ulcer disease d) Mallory-Weiss tear Correct Answer: b) Crohn’s disease Explanation: Crohn's disease is an inflammatory bowel disease that can lead to lower GI bleeding. Other causes of lower GI bleeding include diverticulosis, angiodysplasia, tumors, radiation, and infections. 4. What is the primary treatment goal for a patient presenting with upper GI bleeding in hypovolemic shock? a) Starting vasopressin infusion b) Administering isotonic fluid resuscitation c) Initiating beta blockers d) Performing endoscopy Correct Answer: b) Administering isotonic fluid resuscitation Explanation: Initial management of hypovolemic shock due to upper GI bleeding focuses on isotonic fluid resuscitation to restore intravascular volume and prevent further hemodynamic instability. 5. Which medication used for upper GI bleeding reduces gastric acid secretion and splanchnic blood flow? a) Lactulose b) Beta blockers c) Vasopressin d) Octreotide Correct Answer: d) Octreotide Explanation: Octreotide (Sandostatin) reduces splanchnic blood flow, gastric acid secretion, and GI motility, making it an effective medication for managing upper GI bleeding. 6. A patient with liver disease is experiencing upper GI bleeding. Which medication is critical to administer to prevent ammonia conversion? a) Octreotide b) Vasopressin c) Lactulose d) Beta blockers Correct Answer: c) Lactulose Explanation: Lactulose is an osmotic laxative that removes nitrogenous materials (such as blood) from the gut, thereby preventing the conversion of ammonia—a critical step in patients with liver disease. 7. What is the primary concern when administering vasopressin for upper GI bleeding? a) Risk of hypovolemia b) Decreased portal venous pressure c) Chest pain and ST elevation d) Increased gastric acid secretion Correct Answer: c) Chest pain and ST elevation Explanation: Vasopressin can cause coronary vasoconstriction, leading to chest pain and ST elevation, which requires close monitoring during administration. 8. Which of the following conditions is most likely to require ICU admission for GI bleeding? a) Radiation colitis b) Diverticulosis c) Crohn’s disease d) Upper GI bleeding from esophageal varices Correct Answer: d) Upper GI bleeding from esophageal varices Explanation: Upper GI bleeding, particularly from esophageal varices, often has higher mortality and typically requires ICU admission for intensive management and monitoring. 9. What is the role of beta blockers in managing upper GI bleeding? a) Constricting mesenteric arterioles b) Stimulating gastric acid secretion c) Enhancing clotting factor production d) Increasing portal venous pressure Correct Answer: a) Constricting mesenteric arterioles Explanation: Beta blockers reduce portal venous flow by constricting mesenteric arterioles, thereby reducing the risk of further bleeding in upper GI conditions. 10. A patient presents with upper GI bleeding and signs of liver disease. Which treatment combination is most appropriate to address bleeding and ammonia levels? a) Platelets and corticosteroids b) PRBCs and osmotic diuretics c) Octreotide and lactulose d) Beta blockers and vasopressin Correct Answer: c) Octreotide and lactulose Explanation: Octreotide reduces splanchnic blood flow and gastric acid secretion, while lactulose removes nitrogenous waste to prevent ammonia buildup, addressing both bleeding and liver-related complications. 11. What is the most common cause of esophageal varices? a) Obstruction of the inferior vena cava b) Portal hypertension due to liver disease c) Gallstones in the bile duct d) Thrombosis of the hepatic vein Correct Answer: b) Portal hypertension due to liver disease Explanation: Esophageal varices are caused by increased pressure in the portal vein due to liver cirrhosis, which prevents normal venous drainage through the liver. 12. Which procedure is commonly used to treat esophageal varices and control bleeding? a) Gastric lavage b) Hepatic vein bypass surgery c) Endoscopic banding or sclerosing d) Thoracentesis Correct Answer: c) Endoscopic banding or sclerosing Explanation: Endoscopy with banding or sclerosing of varices is a common intervention to control bleeding and manage esophageal varices effectively. 13. What is the primary safety concern when using an esophageal balloon tamponade to manage bleeding? a) Airway occlusion from balloon displacement b) Rupture of the gastric balloon c) Excessive fluid loss from suction d) Infection at the insertion site Correct Answer: a) Airway occlusion from balloon displacement Explanation: If the esophageal balloon moves upward, it can occlude the airway, causing respiratory distress. In such cases, the esophageal balloon must be cut immediately. 14. What is the primary mechanism of acute pancreatitis? a) Infection by bacterial pathogens b) Obstruction of the portal vein c) Auto-digestion of the pancreas by exocrine enzymes d) Overproduction of insulin and glucagon Correct Answer: c) Auto-digestion of the pancreas by exocrine enzymes Explanation: Acute pancreatitis occurs due to the premature activation of pancreatic exocrine enzymes, leading to inflammation, destruction, and auto-digestion of the pancreas. 15. Which of the following is NOT a common cause of acute pancreatitis? a) Hyperlipidemia b) Gallstones c) Infection d) Alcoholism Correct Answer: c) Infection Explanation: Although infection can occasionally cause acute pancreatitis, it is seldom the primary cause. The most common etiologies are alcoholism, gallstones, and hyperlipidemia. 16. Which exocrine function of the pancreas helps neutralize stomach acid? a) Secretion of trypsin b) Secretion of bicarbonate c) Secretion of lipase d) Secretion of glucagon Correct Answer: b) Secretion of bicarbonate Explanation: The pancreas secretes bicarbonate to neutralize stomach acid and create an optimal environment for digestive enzyme activity in the small intestine. 17. Which of the following is a pulmonary complication of acute pancreatitis? a) ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome) b) Bilateral pleural effusion c) Atelectasis of the right lower lobe d) Pneumothorax Correct Answer: a) ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome) Explanation: Acute pancreatitis can lead to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), resulting in complications like ARDS, left lower lobe atelectasis, and pleural effusion. 18. What stimulates the secretion of pancreatic digestive enzymes? a) Decreased gastric acid production b) Parasympathetic stimulation and ingestion of food c) High levels of glucagon d) Increased sympathetic stimulation Correct Answer: b) Parasympathetic stimulation and ingestion of food Explanation: Parasympathetic stimulation and hormones like secretin and cholecystokinin, released during food ingestion, trigger the pancreas to secrete digestive enzymes. 19. Which type of pancreatic cell is responsible for the secretion of insulin? a) Delta cells b) Acinar cells c) Beta cells d) Alpha cells Correct Answer: c) Beta cells Explanation: Beta cells in the pancreas secrete insulin, which plays a vital role in glucose regulation by lowering blood glucose levels. 20. What is the primary vascular complication in esophageal varices due to liver cirrhosis? a) Increased venous drainage to the inferior vena cava b) Obstruction of the hepatic vein c) Clot formation in the portal vein d) Backflow of blood into the esophageal veins Correct Answer: d) Backflow of blood into the esophageal veins Explanation: Liver cirrhosis increases portal vein pressure, causing backflow of blood into esophageal veins, leading to varices, often referred to as "hemorrhoids of the esophagus." 21. Which symptom is most commonly associated with acute pancreatitis? a) Rebound tenderness in the abdomen b) Abdominal pain radiating to the lumbar area c) High-grade fever d) Increased bowel sounds Correct Answer: b) Abdominal pain radiating to the lumbar area Explanation: Acute pancreatitis typically presents with severe abdominal pain that radiates to all quadrants and the lumbar region. It is often described as "boring" in nature. 22. What lab finding is most specific for diagnosing acute pancreatitis? a) Elevated LDH levels b) Decreased calcium levels c) Elevated amylase and lipase levels d) Elevated WBC count Correct Answer: c) Elevated amylase and lipase levels Explanation: Elevated serum amylase and lipase are key diagnostic markers for acute pancreatitis, with lipase being more specific as it remains elevated longer than amylase. 23. What is the significance of Trousseau’s sign in a patient with acute pancreatitis? a) It reflects hypocalcemia and neuromuscular irritability. b) It suggests ARDS development. c) It signifies surfactant deficiency. d) It indicates hyperglycemia. Correct Answer: a) It reflects hypocalcemia and neuromuscular irritability. Explanation: Trousseau’s sign, elicited during inflation of a blood pressure cuff, is a clinical indicator of hypocalcemia due to calcium being used up in the auto-digestion process in acute pancreatitis. 24. What is Cullen’s sign indicative of in acute pancreatitis? a) Biliary obstruction b) Intra-abdominal bleeding c) Pulmonary effusion d) Retroperitoneal bleeding Correct Answer: b) Intra-abdominal bleeding Explanation: Cullen’s sign, characterized by bluish discoloration around the umbilicus, is a sign of intra-abdominal bleeding caused by methemalbumin tracking around the abdomen. 25. Which of the following findings is associated with severe retroperitoneal bleeding in acute pancreatitis? a) Grey Turner’s sign b) Positive rebound tenderness c) Cullen’s sign d) Trousseau’s sign Correct Answer: a) Grey Turner’s sign Explanation: Grey Turner’s sign, characterized by bluish discoloration of the flanks, indicates retroperitoneal bleeding, often seen in hemorrhagic pancreatitis. 26. Which of the following is a critical lab value in Ranson’s criteria at admission for acute pancreatitis? a) LDH > 350 IU/L b) AST < 250 U/L c) Glucose < 200 mg/dL d) WBC < 16,000/mm³ Correct Answer: a) LDH > 350 IU/L Explanation: Ranson’s criteria at admission include LDH > 350 IU/L as a marker for assessing the severity and prognosis of acute pancreatitis. 27. What does a hematocrit decrease of more than 10% within 48 hours suggest in a patient with acute pancreatitis? a) Decreased risk of complications b) Pancreatic necrosis c) Severe intravascular fluid loss d) Persistent inflammation Correct Answer: c) Severe intravascular fluid loss Explanation: A significant drop in hematocrit reflects fluid sequestration and severe dehydration, indicating a poor prognosis in acute pancreatitis. 28. What pulmonary complication is most commonly associated with acute pancreatitis? a) Pulmonary hypertension b) Pneumothorax c) ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome) d) Pleural effusion in the right lung Correct Answer: c) ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome) Explanation: Acute pancreatitis can lead to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), causing ARDS as a result of damage to type II alveolar cells and reduced surfactant production. 29. What is the clinical significance of hypocalcemia in acute pancreatitis? a) It increases the risk of hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS). b) It reduces the production of pancreatic enzymes. c) It contributes to neuromuscular excitability and prolonged QT intervals. d) It improves fluid sequestration. Correct Answer: c) It contributes to neuromuscular excitability and prolonged QT intervals. Explanation: Hypocalcemia in acute pancreatitis results from calcium being consumed during auto-digestion, leading to signs like Trousseau’s sign, prolonged QT intervals, and seizures. 30. According to Ranson’s criteria, what is the threshold PaO₂ value during the first 48 hours that indicates severe pancreatitis? a) < 50 mmHg b) < 60 mmHg c) > 80 mmHg d) > 70 mmHg Correct Answer: b) < 60 mmHg Explanation: A PaO₂ value of less than 60 mmHg during the first 48 hours is one of Ranson’s criteria and indicates severe pancreatitis with increased morbidity and mortality risk. 31. What is a key component of the initial treatment for acute pancreatitis? a) Antibiotics b) Enteral feeding above the duodenum c) Surgery to remove the pancreas d) Fluid and electrolyte replacement Correct Answer: d) Fluid and electrolyte replacement Explanation: Acute pancreatitis requires aggressive fluid resuscitation and replacement of electrolytes like calcium, potassium, and magnesium to address the systemic inflammatory response and prevent complications. 32. Which medication is used to manage pain in acute pancreatitis? a) Ibuprofen b) Morphine c) Acetaminophen d) Aspirin Correct Answer: b) Morphine Explanation: Morphine is the preferred analgesic for managing severe pain associated with acute pancreatitis, ensuring adequate relief while monitoring for complications. 33. What is the primary cause of acute hepatic failure in the United States? a) Alcohol abuse b) Viral hepatitis c) Acetaminophen overdose d) Autoimmune liver disease Correct Answer: c) Acetaminophen overdose Explanation: Acetaminophen toxicity is the leading cause of acute hepatic failure, often due to unintentional overdose or suicide attempts, resulting in fulminant hepatitis. 34. Which of the following lab findings is most commonly associated with hepatic failure? a) Elevated ammonia levels b) Elevated serum albumin c) Decreased serum bilirubin d) Normal prothrombin time (PT) Correct Answer: a) Elevated ammonia levels Explanation: Elevated ammonia levels are a hallmark of hepatic failure, contributing to hepatic encephalopathy and neurological symptoms such as asterixis. 35. Asterixis, seen in hepatic failure, is characterized by which of the following? a) Jerky leg movements b) Flapping tremor of the hands c) Muscle rigidity d) Rapid eye movement Correct Answer: b) Flapping tremor of the hands Explanation: Asterixis is a flapping tremor of the hands, often triggered by elevated ammonia levels in hepatic encephalopathy. 36. Which clinical feature distinguishes Stage IV hepatic encephalopathy? a) Areflexia and positive Babinski sign b) Mild confusion and irritability c) Stupor and hyperactive reflexes d) Asterixis and abnormal EEG Correct Answer: a) Areflexia and positive Babinski sign Explanation: Stage IV hepatic encephalopathy is the most severe stage, characterized by the absence of reflexes except for pathologic ones, including the Babinski sign, and unresponsiveness to stimuli. 37. What is the primary risk associated with ascites in hepatic failure? a) ARDS b) Pulmonary embolism c) Pancreatitis d) Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis Correct Answer: d) Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis Explanation: Ascites, a common finding in hepatic failure, predisposes patients to spontaneous bacterial peritonitis due to low albumin levels and compromised immune function. 38. Which electrolyte abnormality is most likely in a patient with acute hepatic failure? a) Hypernatremia b) Hypercalcemia c) Hyperkalemia d) Hypoglycemia Correct Answer: d) Hypoglycemia Explanation: In hepatic failure, the liver’s inability to perform gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis often results in hypoglycemia, requiring close glucose monitoring. 39. What is the primary cause of renal failure in hepatic failure? a) Hypovolemia b) Hepatorenal syndrome c) Electrolyte imbalance d) Direct liver injury Correct Answer: b) Hepatorenal syndrome Explanation: Hepatorenal syndrome is a functional renal failure associated with advanced liver disease, characterized by vasoconstriction of renal arteries and high mortality. 40. Which lab abnormality is typically a late finding in hepatic failure? a) Elevated AST and ALT b) Increased PT and PTT c) Elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) d) Decreased serum bilirubin Correct Answer: c) Elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) Explanation: Elevated BUN and creatinine are late findings in hepatic failure, often indicating renal dysfunction secondary to hepatorenal syndrome or sepsis. 41. What is the primary goal in managing hepatic encephalopathy? a) Administering lactated Ringer’s solution b) Restricting all protein intake c) Preventing and reducing serum ammonia levels d) Increasing dietary potassium Correct Answer: c) Preventing and reducing serum ammonia levels Explanation: The main goal in managing hepatic encephalopathy is to prevent and reduce factors that elevate serum ammonia, as elevated NH₃ levels worsen the condition and can increase intracranial pressure. 42. Which medication is used to eliminate ammonia-producing bacteria in the gut during hepatic encephalopathy? a) Clotting factors b) Neomycin c) Acetylcysteine d) Aldactone Correct Answer: b) Neomycin Explanation: Neomycin is an antibiotic used to kill gut bacteria that produce ammonia, thereby helping to lower serum ammonia levels. A potential complication of its use is vitamin K deficiency. 43. Why should lactated Ringer’s be avoided in patients with hepatic failure? a) It can trigger lactic acidosis. b) It directly raises ammonia levels. c) It contains excess potassium. d) It increases protein breakdown. Correct Answer: a) It can trigger lactic acidosis. Explanation: Lactated Ringer’s is normally converted to bicarbonate in a healthy liver. In hepatic failure, this process is impaired, leading to lactic acidosis, which worsens ammonia levels and hepatic encephalopathy. 44. What is a common complication of the TIPS procedure? a) Esophageal varices rupture b) Increased portal hypertension c) Ascites d) Hepatic encephalopathy Correct Answer: d) Hepatic encephalopathy Explanation: The TIPS procedure creates a shunt that bypasses the liver, reducing portal hypertension but decreasing the liver’s ability to detoxify blood, leading to increased serum ammonia and hepatic encephalopathy. 45. What is the rationale for using Aldactone in hepatic failure? a) It decreases serum protein. b) It enhances clotting factor production. c) It prevents hypokalemia. d) It reduces serum ammonia directly. Correct Answer: c) It prevents hypokalemia. Explanation: Aldactone, a potassium-sparing diuretic, is used to treat ascites in hepatic failure while preventing hypokalemia, which can trigger ammoniagenesis and worsen hepatic encephalopathy. 46. Which symptom is most indicative of splenic rupture? a) Sharp left shoulder pain (Kehr’s sign) b) Bluish discoloration of the flanks c) Cyanosis around the umbilical area d) Absent deep tendon reflexes Correct Answer: a) Sharp left shoulder pain (Kehr’s sign) Explanation: Kehr’s sign, or sharp pain in the left shoulder, occurs due to diaphragmatic irritation from blood leaking into the peritoneal cavity, commonly seen in splenic rupture. 47. What dietary restriction is typically advised during hepatic encephalopathy? a) Low sodium b) Low protein c) Low glucose d) Low potassium Correct Answer: b) Low protein Explanation: Protein restriction is advised in hepatic encephalopathy to reduce the nitrogenous waste products that contribute to elevated serum ammonia levels. 48. Which of the following is a possible side effect of neomycin therapy in hepatic failure? a) Metabolic alkalosis b) Hyperkalemia c) Elevated glucose d) Vitamin K deficiency Correct Answer: d) Vitamin K deficiency Explanation: Neomycin can disrupt gut flora, reducing the production of vitamin K by bacteria, which may lead to clotting issues. 49. What is the significance of elevated BUN in hepatic failure? a) It signals acute kidney injury. b) It reflects an excess of potassium. c) It indicates hypovolemia. d) It increases serum ammonia levels. Correct Answer: d) It increases serum ammonia levels. Explanation: Elevated BUN results from protein breakdown, which contributes to increased ammonia production, exacerbating hepatic encephalopathy. 50. What immune-related complication is commonly seen in patients with a history of splenectomy? a) Overactive B-cell immunity b) Increased platelet production c) Reduced resistance to infections d) Excessive inflammation in the liver Correct Answer: c) Reduced resistance to infections Explanation: The spleen plays a key role in filtering blood and supporting immune function. Its removal significantly increases susceptibility to infections. 51. Which clinical sign is most indicative of a ruptured spleen? a) Grey Turner’s sign b) Absent bowel sounds c) Kehr’s sign d) Cullen’s sign Correct Answer: c) Kehr’s sign Explanation: Kehr’s sign refers to sharp left shoulder pain caused by diaphragmatic irritation from blood leaking into the peritoneal cavity, commonly seen in splenic rupture. 52. What does Grey Turner’s sign indicate? a) Splenic rupture b) Diaphragmatic rupture c) Retroperitoneal bleeding d) Intraperitoneal bleeding Correct Answer: c) Retroperitoneal bleeding Explanation: Grey Turner’s sign is ecchymosis of the flanks, which is a clinical indicator of retroperitoneal bleeding. 53. What is the significance of bowel sounds heard in the chest after abdominal trauma? a) Diaphragmatic rupture b) Bowel perforation c) Intraperitoneal bleeding d) Splenic rupture Correct Answer: a) Diaphragmatic rupture Explanation: Bowel sounds in the chest suggest a diaphragmatic rupture, allowing abdominal contents to move into the thoracic cavity. 54. At what intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) level is intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH) diagnosed? a) >12–15 mmHg b) >8 mmHg c) >18 mmHg d) >20 mmHg Correct Answer: a) >12–15 mmHg Explanation: Intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH) is defined as an IAP greater than 12–15 mmHg, and it may lead to compromised organ perfusion. 55. What condition is associated with a sustained intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) of >20 mmHg and new organ dysfunction? a) Intra-abdominal hypertension b) Abdominal compartment syndrome c) Intraperitoneal bleeding d) Retroperitoneal bleeding Correct Answer: b) Abdominal compartment syndrome Explanation: Abdominal compartment syndrome is defined as a sustained IAP of >20 mmHg, often accompanied by new organ dysfunction or failure. 56. What is the best method for measuring intra-abdominal pressure (IAP)? a) Measuring bladder pressure b) Palpating the abdomen c) Conducting an ultrasound d) Performing a CT scan Correct Answer: a) Measuring bladder pressure Explanation: Bladder pressure measurement closely approximates intraperitoneal pressure and is considered the most accurate method for assessing IAP. 57. Which intervention is appropriate for managing an intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) of ≥12 mmHg? a) Elevate the head of the bed above 30 degrees. b) Place the patient in reverse Trendelenburg position. c) Increase intravenous fluid administration. d) Perform decompressive laparotomy. What is the abdominal perfusion pressure (APP) threshold associated with improved survival? a) >60 mmHg b) >70 mmHg c) >50 mmHg d) >40 mmHg Correct Answer: a) >60 mmHg Explanation: An APP of 60 mmHg or greater is associated with improved survival by ensuring adequate perfusion of abdominal organs. 59. What clinical intervention is considered for an intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) exceeding 20 mmHg? a) Loosening abdominal binders b) Administering additional fluids c) Decompression surgery d) Starting gastroprokinetic agents Correct Answer: c) Decompression surgery Explanation: Decompression surgery, or laparotomy, is indicated for an IAP exceeding 20 mmHg, especially if there is evidence of organ dysfunction or failure. 60. What does Cullen’s sign indicate in a trauma patient? a) Retroperitoneal bleeding b) Intraperitoneal bleeding c) Bowel perforation d) Splenic injury Correct Answer: b) Intraperitoneal bleeding Explanation: Cullen’s sign, or ecchymosis around the umbilicus, is a clinical sign of intraperitoneal bleeding. 61. Which vitamin deficiency is most commonly associated with bariatric surgery? a) Vitamin C b) Vitamin A c) Vitamin B-12 d) Vitamin D Correct Answer: c) Vitamin B-12 Explanation: Bariatric surgery often leads to malabsorption of essential nutrients, including vitamin B-12, due to reduced gastric acid and intrinsic factor required for its absorption. 62. What is the most common cause of gallstones after bariatric surgery? a) Hypercalcemia b) Protein deficiency c) Bile stasis d) Intestinal obstruction Correct Answer: c) Bile stasis Explanation: Gallstones after bariatric surgery result from bile stasis due to rapid weight loss and reduced fat intake, which decrease gallbladder motility. 63. What is the primary artery responsible for perfusion to the small intestine? a) Celiac artery b) Renal artery c) Inferior mesenteric artery d) Superior mesenteric artery Correct Answer: d) Superior mesenteric artery Explanation: The superior mesenteric artery supplies arterial blood to the small intestine and is crucial for maintaining its perfusion. 64. Which clinical symptom is most suggestive of bowel infarction? a) Absent abdominal distension b) Severe cramping abdominal pain c) High-pitched bowel sounds d) Projectile vomiting Correct Answer: b) Severe cramping abdominal pain Explanation: Severe, cramping abdominal pain, often periumbilical or diffuse, is a hallmark symptom of bowel infarction due to ischemia. 65. What is the typical bowel sound pattern in early small bowel obstruction? a) Gurgling bowel sounds b) High-pitched bowel sounds c) Low-pitched bowel sounds d) Absent bowel sounds Correct Answer: b) High-pitched bowel sounds Explanation: High-pitched bowel sounds are characteristic of early small bowel obstruction due to increased peristaltic activity attempting to overcome the obstruction. 71.
